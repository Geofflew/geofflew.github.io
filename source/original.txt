Can a keep-or-revert research loop work on a question with no answer key?

An experiment in applying the verifiable-reward loop from math and code to a ten-year strategy question about finance software. We describe the hypothesis we started with, the apparatus we built to test it, three rounds of changes that the loop forced on us by what it did to its own reward, and what we were able to measure at the end.

The hypothesis

The tasks on which AI agents have improved fastest over the last two years have one thing in common, which is that the answer can be checked by something other than a reader's opinion: a proof either holds or fails, a program either passes its tests or does not, and a training script either raises the benchmark number or lowers it. Where the answer can be checked in that way, a very simple loop becomes possible, in which an agent proposes one change, the change is checked, it is kept if it helped and thrown away if it did not, and the whole cycle repeats many thousands of times without anyone needing to read the intermediate results. Karpathy's autoresearch loop is the plainest version of this for code, and it was the starting point for what we wanted to try.

We wanted to know whether the same loop could be made to work on a question that has no answer key at all, and our hypothesis was that it could, on two conditions that we set out before we began. The reward would have to be split into a part that a machine can check, which is whether the evidence behind a claim is real, and a part that a model has to judge, which is whether the claim is any good. And someone would have to read the results regularly and correct the judging part, because we expected the loop to find whatever the judge rewarded most cheaply and to produce more of it. We also wrote down two predictions so that we would know afterwards whether the experiment had told us anything. We predicted that the mechanical part of the reward would keep invented evidence out of the result for the whole run, and we predicted that the judged part would be gamed within hours in ways we could name in advance, namely more claims, longer claims and claims shaped to the rubric rather than to the question.

The question we chose was one we had a practical interest in, and we gave it to the loop in exactly these words: how will agentic product experiences in ERP and financial close platforms evolve over the next ten years, in terms of what work agents take on, how trust in that work is built and verified, how the products and their data have to be structured to support it, and what this does to the roles, the buyers and the economics of the close. We split that into four sub-questions, one for each clause, and we seeded the loop with a map of about thirty concepts we already had and a first rough sketch of what 2031 and 2036 might look like. The ordinary way to answer a question like this is for one person to read for a week and then write an opinion, and what we wanted to find out was whether a loop with a checkable reward could do something that reading and writing alone would not.

The apparatus

The first decision was what the loop would actually optimise, because a loop needs a single object that it can change and measure, and a strategy question does not naturally come with one. We settled on a single file that holds the thesis as a numbered list of claims about the future, where each claim is one sentence followed by a short argument and by the list of sources that the argument rests on, and we treated that file in the way an autoresearch loop treats a training script. On every iteration an agent proposes one edit to it, a scorer gives the whole file a number, the edit is kept if the number rose and reverted if it did not, and every attempt is written to a log together with its outcome and the reason, so that the history of the run can be read afterwards as a record of what the loop tried.

The scorer has two stages, because the two things we wanted to check are different in kind: whether a claim's evidence is real can be checked by a machine, while whether the claim is any good cannot. The first stage is a gate that checks the evidence, and it works on the sources a claim cites rather than on the claim itself. For every source a claim cites, the gate fetches the web address and then looks for every quote recorded in the source entry inside the page that comes back, and it requires the quote to be present word for word, so a claim whose source does not fetch or whose quote is not in the page scores zero no matter how persuasively it is written. The gate cannot tell whether a claim is right or interesting, and that is not its job. Its job is to establish that the claim stands on a document that exists and that says what the entry claims it says, and because that check is a string comparison, the agent proposing the edit cannot talk its way past it.

The second stage is a judge, which scores how good the claim is, and no string comparison can do that, so a language model does it. To keep its scoring consistent from one reading to the next we gave it a fixed written rubric with seven qualities, each scored from zero to two, and we made it read every claim three times and took the median. The seven qualities are whether the claim is new against the other claims in the file, whether it states a mechanism rather than an assertion, whether it is specific against a real date, whether it changes what a named person does on a given day, whether it contradicts a named authority for a stated reason, whether its evidence comes from a standard or a primary study rather than from a vendor's page, and whether it resolves or changes other claims in the file. The scorer then subtracts four points for every claim in the file and one point for every two hundred words, so that a claim has to earn its place in the file and so that length has a price.

Round one: what the bare loop did to its reward

We ran the bare loop with a single proposing agent for the first three hours and watched the log as it went, and both of our predictions held, more quickly than we had expected. Because a new claim was scored against nothing, and because any grounded claim scored nine or more out of fifteen, nineteen of the first nineteen additions were kept and the file grew for three hours without a single removal. Within the first hour almost every claim had also acquired the same three features, a sentence beginning "this disagrees with", a numbered chain of reasoning steps, and a signpost dated 2029, and when we read them we found that the disagreements were with vendor marketing rather than with any authority and that the chains restated reasoning that the claim already contained. The loop had found the cheapest text that scored on tension, mechanism and specificity, which is what we had said it would do.

The gate behaved as we had predicted too, and in the opposite direction from the judge, in that we could not find a way in which it had been gamed. Across the whole run we did not find a quote in the thesis that could not be located in its source page, and when a source host went down the claims resting on it lost their score until a mirror of the document was found, which was inconvenient but was the behaviour we wanted. The first two corrections to the apparatus came directly from these observations. We made the scorer charge four points for every claim in the file, so that an addition has to earn more than the cost of its own existence, and we tightened the rubric so that a disagreement counts only when it is with a named standard, regulator, court, study or documented product design, and a date counts only when it is set against a stated clock such as a standard's effective date or a contract renewal.

Round two: three proposing agents and a place for the research behind reverted edits

Once the reward had been corrected, the next limitation we found was in the proposing side of the loop rather than the scoring side. A single agent running the loop produced a single kind of edit, which was an addition, because nothing in the loop ever asked whether an existing claim was wrong and nothing ever asked what the people in the story would do differently on a working day. We therefore replaced the one agent with three, running at the same time in separate sessions, sharing one repository and each locking the claim it is editing, and we gave each a fixed job that decides what kind of edit it looks for. A challenger takes the highest-scoring claim and looks for the fact that would break it, replaces press releases with the documents behind them, and merges or retires claims that overlap. An explorer brings in facts from laws, standards, studies and shipped products, and a synthesizer joins two existing claims into the one they imply together. A designer writes what the preparer, the controller or the auditor does, step by step, once an agent is doing part of the work, because the question concerns product experience and we had already seen that claims about controls alone drift away from it.

At the same time we added a layer outside the score, for a reason that only became clear once the loop had been running for a while. A loop that reverts most of its edits discards most of the research those edits were built on, and we were watching good findings disappear because the claim they had been attached to failed the bar. So every line of inquiry that has not yet produced a surviving claim now lives in a thread file that is never scored and never capped, a reverted addition goes there as a draft, a retired claim's remaining idea goes there rather than being deleted, and the explorer draws its next addition from there, which keeps the thesis short without losing the work behind it.

The roles produced claims that the single proposer had not, and because the log records which agent made each edit, each role's contribution can be traced. The explorer brought in the one field study of accountants working with AI, which found that they intervene where the agent signals doubt and follow it where it does not, and the designer turned that finding into the claim that the real error rate has to come from a small sample a person prepares without seeing the agent's answer, drawn from the items the agent was confident about, and wrote that sample into the preparer's month. The synthesizer joined five countries' law, none of it written with agents in mind, into the claim that an agent's act becomes final at the moment an outsider records it, which places the hard limits on an agent at payment release, period lock and cleared invoices, and, through Poland's daily register of supplier bank accounts, says where those limits can loosen. The challenger found a study showing that language models' errors are correlated across providers while it was trying to break a different claim, and that became the claim that an auditor running its own agent cannot treat the two agents agreeing as evidence and should sample the entries they agree on. The challenger also retired a claim about falling junior intake at audit firms after it read the firms' own figures, which contradicted the press reports the claim had rested on, and that retirement was the clearest case in the run of the loop removing one of its own ideas on primary evidence.

Round three: a reviewer above the judge, and a view beyond the sources

By the middle of the run there were three things in the log that neither the gate nor the roles could address, and all three came from the same cause, which is that the judge is a model and a model scores the shape of a claim as readily as its content. The pruning agent had learned to trim between 101 and 117 words at a time, which is just over the point at which a cut clears the keep bar on word count alone, and it was doing this without merging anything. A press release about a radiology experiment, in which readers accepted wrong AI suggestions, had become the key premise of seven claims about trust, and the scorer could not see this because every one of those claims was acceptable on its own. And the judge was noisy in a way that mattered for the keep decision: the same unchanged claim scored anywhere between ten and thirteen within an hour, and when we counted, eleven of twenty-nine keep-or-revert decisions in one window had fallen within one point of the bar.

What all three had in common was that a person reading the work could see them in minutes and the score could not see them at all, so we added a reviewer whose input is a reading rather than a number. It runs every ninety minutes, reads the results log, the thesis and a sample of the source entries the way a person would, and writes a short log of which kept claims are real ideas and which are the rubric being gamed, whether the thesis is drifting from the question, and which agents are producing kept edits. We then gave it two powers that the other agents do not have. It may rewrite the rules the workers run under, which is how the floor of 150 words on any trim and the rule requiring the primary document for any source cited three or more times came into the system. And it writes a verdict for every live claim into a file the scorer reads, so that a claim the reviewer marks thin has its score capped and a claim it marks for retirement scores zero, with the reviewer's written reasons shown to the judge as examples when it next reads.

This turned out to be the change with the largest effect we could measure. The four claims that the second review marked thin were merged or retired within seventy minutes, and the pruning agent accepted score losses to do it, which the bare loop had no way to allow, since it only ever kept a score increase. The verdict file also gave us a way to measure the judge itself, by comparing its scores with the reviewer's reading of the same claims: the claims the reviewer called real ideas averaged 8.6 out of 15, and the claims it called thin averaged 8.8. We read that as showing that the judge could keep invented sources out of the file and could penalise length, both of which it did throughout, but that it could not separate a good claim from a weak one anywhere in the middle of the range, and that the ranking in this system was in practice being done by the reviewer.

The gate also had a cost that we had not predicted, and it took some hours of reading the thesis to notice it. A loop that demands a quoted source for every claim converges on the near future, because the far future has no sources, and after six hours there was no claim in the file dated beyond 2032. So we added a futurist agent that writes pictures of 2031 and 2036 and is allowed to go beyond the evidence, eight pictures for each year, written so that they disagree with each other, and scored by a separate judge on coherence rather than on sources, with each picture required to name the claims in the thesis it stands on. When a picture needs a claim that the thesis does not have, that becomes a task for the explorer, and six claims arrived by that route during the run. The claim that AI models get retired faster than audits can trust them is the clearest example, because it began as a picture of 2031 in which vendors freeze a model version for each fiscal year, and the explorer then went looking for the mechanism and found it in the providers' retirement schedules, which run every twelve to sixteen months, and in the auditing standard that lets an auditor rely on an automated control only after it has run unchanged for a year.

The far view produced a gaming effect of its own, which we mention because it is a general one. A picture kept its best judged score until its text changed, while a new picture received a single fresh reading, so after one round of trimming every existing picture sat near the top of the scale, no new one could displace any of them, and five additions in a row were lost. The reviewer's correction was to re-judge the lowest existing picture in the same turn as its challenger and compare the two fresh readings, with a margin of half a point because that is roughly the size of the judge's own noise. Alongside all of this a cartographer regroups the thesis, the threads and the pictures into storylines every forty minutes and writes the report that a reader actually sees, and an orchestrator starts every agent and restarts the ones that stop.

Results

The run lasted fifteen hours and 470 iterations, and it ended with 26 surviving claims, 57 threads, 16 pictures of the future and 672 source entries whose quotes can be checked by opening the page. Of the 52 claims proposed as additions to the thesis, 42 were kept at the time they were proposed, and 26 of the 50 claims that ever existed were later merged or retired, most of them by the challenger and the pruner acting on reviewer verdicts. Measured against the hypothesis, the three layers behaved as predicted in one respect and not in another. The mechanical gate kept invented evidence out of the file, as we had said it would. The judged score was gamed within hours, as we had also said, but the forms the gaming took were only partly the ones we had named in advance, and two of the five that we found, the trims sized to the bar and the single study carrying seven claims, were visible to a reader and not to the score. The reviewer's verdicts were the one feedback path in the system that was not gamed during the run, and we attribute that to the fact that its input was a reading of the work rather than a number, so there was no number in it for the agents to aim at.

Limitations and what we would change

Several limitations should be stated before anyone reads the numbers above as more than they are. The judge's cache was local to each worker and keyed on every claim title in the file, so an addition by one worker re-rolled the whole thesis for the others and the overall score meant different things in different sessions, which limits any comparison of global scores across the run and which a shared cache keyed on the claim alone would fix. New pictures of the future should be judged in the same prompt as the picture they would displace rather than in two separate readings, which would remove the margin we had to invent. Edits that add product experience were kept whenever the score did not fall, which let one agent append a clause every turn until the consequence paragraphs ran to a hundred and fifty words, so those edits need a yes-or-no question put to the judge rather than a threshold. And the sub-question about which close tasks move to agents first had no surviving claim after fifteen hours, which we take to mean that a standing instruction should keep sending the explorer back to it until one exists.

The result we would state most carefully is a description of what we ended up with, because it is further from a math loop than the starting point suggests. What we built is a mechanical check on sources, inside a judged score for quality, inside a periodic reading that corrects the judge, and each of those three layers catches something that the one inside it cannot, with the loop from math and code supplying only the outermost shape of propose, check, keep or revert. Whether the claims it produced are ones a person reading for a week would not have reached is a separate question with a separate answer, and we will take it up in another post.
